## The Army Before the Campaign {#the-army}

The Army of Italy in early 1796 was the most wretched force in the French Republic's service. Garrisoned along the Ligurian Riviera — stretched thin from Nice to Genoa — the soldiers endured conditions that would have broken a less hardened army.

### Starvation and Neglect

- The Directory considered Italy a **sideshow** — the real war was on the Rhine. The Army of Italy got the dregs of everything.
- Soldiers went days without bread. Some units hadn't been paid in months, possibly over a year.
- Pay came in worthless *mandats territoriaux* (paper currency). A soldier's 15 sous daily bought nothing.
- Uniforms rotted off men's backs. Entire battalions lacked shoes. Some men lacked muskets.
- Hospitals overwhelmed and undersupplied. Disease — malaria, dysentery, scurvy — thinned ranks as much as combat.
- Desertion constant, yet a remarkable number stayed — bound by revolutionary conviction, unit loyalty, or having nowhere else to go.

Napoleon wrote to the Directory (28 March 1796):
> *"The army lacks everything — bread, meat, clothing, shoes. The soldiers are dying of hunger and disease. Discipline is dissolving."*

### Strength

- **On paper**: ~106,000 men
- **Effective**: ~63,000
- **Available for field operations**: ~37,600 men and 60 field guns
- Artillery scarce, cavalry weak (bad terrain for horses, mounts broken down)

### Spirit

Despite everything, this was a **republican army**. Many had enlisted out of genuine revolutionary conviction. They had defeated the armies of kings. They were citizens-soldiers, not the slaves of a monarch. But four years of war and broken promises had bred cynicism about the politicians in Paris.

A famous (possibly apocryphal) exchange: when a staff officer complained about the army's condition, a common soldier replied, *"We have no bread, but we have bayonets."*

The demi-brigade was a soldier's world. The sergent was the most important authority figure in his life. The men who shared his campfire and his bread (when there was bread) were closer than family.

## Napoleon Takes Command {#napoleon-arrives}

### The Appointment

**2 March 1796**: The Directory appoints Napoleon Bonaparte, age 26, to command the Army of Italy. General Schérer, the outgoing commander, had asked to be relieved — he reportedly suggested the "26-year-old upstart Corsican" who wrote the campaign plan from Paris should come execute it himself.

Napoleon married Joséphine de Beauharnais on 9 March and left Paris for the front two days later.

### Arrival at Nice (25-27 March)

Napoleon arrived at headquarters in Nice. He was thin, sallow, with lank hair — notably short. The reaction of his three senior division commanders was contemptuous.

**Augereau** (tall, rough, over six feet) dismissed him as "this little bastard of a general" who'd won his rank through politics. After the first briefing, Augereau left shaken:
> *"This little bastard of a general actually frightened me. I can't understand the ascendancy he exercises over me from the very first moment."*

**Masséna's** famous account:
> *"He put on his general's hat and seemed to have grown two feet."*

### The Proclamation

Napoleon addressed his soldiers (traditionally dated 28 March, likely polished after the fact):

> *"Soldiers, you are naked, ill-fed! The Government owes you much; it can give you nothing. Your patience, the courage you display in the midst of these rocks, are admirable; but they procure you no glory, no fame is reflected upon you. I seek to lead you into the most fertile plains in the world. Rich provinces, great cities will be in your power. There you will find honor, glory, and riches."*

Not ideological abstractions — **loot, food, shoes, and glory.**

### Immediate Impact

- Enforced discipline, broke up rotten units
- Berthier reorganized supply, scoured southern France for food/transport/forage
- Moved headquarters forward from Nice to **Albenga** (~4 April), then to **Savona** (9 April)
- Arrived with a detailed campaign plan he'd been refining since 1794

## Why Voltri? {#why-voltri}

### How the French Got There

The French presence at Voltri was a political scheme gone wrong. **Saliceti**, the Directory's representative, tried to bully the Republic of Genoa into a loan. General Schérer ordered ~5,000 men east toward Genoa to intimidate the Genoese. General **Pijon** marched from Savona with the 51st and 75th Line demi-brigades on 24 March.

Napoleon replaced Schérer on 27 March and ordered the movement suspended — but troops were already at Voltri. Pijon fell ill and was replaced by General **Cervoni**, a Corsican friend of the Bonaparte family.

Napoleon didn't consider Voltri strategically important. It was an exposed outpost, far from his main concentration at Savona. But the troops were already there, and their presence served a useful purpose: **bait**.

### Beaulieu Takes the Bait

**General Beaulieu** (Austrian commander, age 70) was convinced the French objective was Genoa. The French brigade at Voltri confirmed his theory. He planned a two-pronged attack to destroy the Voltri garrison and link up with the British fleet.

**The fatal blunder**: By moving his left wing over the mountains to attack Voltri, Beaulieu placed an entire mountain range between himself and his right wing under **Argenteau** (~8,000 men near Dego). Napoleon simply ignored the coastal attack and struck at the exposed center.

Beaulieu won the battle. He lost the campaign.

## The Battle of Voltri (10 April 1796) {#the-battle}

### French Garrison (~5,000 men)

Commander: **General of Brigade Jean-Baptiste Cervoni** (age 31, Corsican)

- **75th Line Infantry Demi-Brigade** (~3,181 men) under Chef de brigade **Chambarlhac** — positioned at **Pegli**, covering the coastal approach and Bocchetta Pass road
- **Two battalions, 51st Line Infantry Demi-Brigade** (~2,000 men) with **three companies of converged grenadiers** led by Chef de brigade **Jean Lannes** (age 27) — positioned at **Mele**, covering the Turchino Pass road
- Divisional train at Voltri town

*Note: The army had just undergone reorganization — the 51st was formerly the 99th, the 75th formerly the 70th. Unit cohesion may have been loose.*

### Austrian Attack (~7,200 men in two columns)

**Western Column — FML Sebottendorf** (~3,200 men), Beaulieu personally accompanying:
- Wenzel Colloredo Infantry Regiment Nr. 56 (2 bns)
- Carlstädter Grenz Infantry Regiment (1 bn)
- Alvinczi Infantry Regiment (1 bn)
- Advance guard under **Oberst Vukassovich**
- Route: through the **Turchino Pass** (588m), descending toward Mele

**Eastern Column — GM Pittoni** (~3,975 men):
- Reisky IR Nr. 13 (2 bns), Terzi IR Nr. 16 (1 bn), Nádásdy IR Nr. 39 (1 bn), Szluiner Grenz IR Nr. 63 (1 bn)
- Meszáros Uhlans (4 squadrons, 624 cavalry)
- Route: through the **Bocchetta Pass**, descending from Campomorone

### Timeline

**9 April** — 75th Line skirmishes all day against Austrian probing forces. Tension rising.

**10 April:**

- **~11:00 AM** — Pittoni's main body finally marches from Campomorone. Late start, poor coordination.
- **~2:00 PM** — Sebottendorf's column clears French outpost from Masone. Vukassovich leads advance, sends Grenzers against Acquasanta.
- **~3:00 PM** — Pittoni's advance guard (Grenzer companies under Captain **Maelcamp**, Beaulieu's son-in-law) presses the 75th near Pegli. **Two hills taken by bayonet.**
- **3:00-6:00 PM** — Intense skirmishing. The **75th holds Pegli for three hours**. Four companies briefly surrounded but break out.
- **~5:00 PM** — Inland, French fall back to fortification at Mele. **Lannes conducts fighting withdrawal** with 51st elements. Vukassovich threatens to turn Cervoni's left flank.
  - Vivid account: Cervoni sees the 51st running for Mele's buildings. Lannes rides past, **cursing every French soldier he passes**. Skirmishers try to slow Vukassovich while another battalion forms against disordered Uhlans. A nearby 51st battalion "promptly bolted for the rear."
- **~6:00 PM** — 75th withdraws from Pegli to avoid encirclement, falls back along coastal road.
- **~7:00 PM** — Bulk of French force shakes off Austrian pursuit. Cervoni conducts a **"masterly withdrawal to the west, eluding the trap."**
- **Evening** — Pittoni occupies Voltri. Beaulieu arrives around midnight.

### Casualties

- **French**: ~220 total (75th: 1 officer killed, 7 wounded/missing, 16 killed, 45 wounded, 148 captured. 51st: 2 killed, 6 wounded, 2 missing — very light, reflecting Lannes's skilled withdrawal)
- **Austrian**: ~50 (reflecting the skirmish nature — this was a withdrawal, not a pitched battle)

### Character of the Fighting

This was **not a grand battle**. It was an afternoon of skirmishing, confusion, and retreat:
- Grenzer sharpshooters on the heights picking off exposed soldiers
- Austrian Uhlans (lancers) appearing on roads, causing panic
- Brief, sharp bayonet charges for hilltop positions
- The constant tension of being nearly cut off
- Officers screaming to hold formation while men broke and ran
- Lannes physically riding through his fleeing troops, cursing them back into line

## The Retreat to Savona {#the-retreat}

### The March

Cervoni's brigade fell back **west along the coastal road** from Voltri to Savona — roughly **40 km (25 miles)** of Ligurian coastline. The road hugged the shore, with steep hills pressing close on the inland side.

The retreat was **orderly but tense**. Not a rout — a disciplined withdrawal under pressure. Cervoni's skill in extracting his force intact was noted by historians.

### What a Soldier Would Experience

- **Exhaustion**: Men who'd been skirmishing since the previous day, now marching through the night
- **Uncertainty**: Where was the rest of the army? Were the Austrians pursuing? Were they cut off?
- **The coastal road**: Narrow, winding, mountains on one side, sea on the other. Nowhere to hide if cavalry caught up.
- **Stragglers**: Men falling out from exhaustion, wounds, hunger. The eternal problem of retreat.
- **Relief**: Reaching Savona and finding Napoleon's army assembling, not running.

### Arrival at Savona

Napoleon was unconcerned by the loss of Voltri. It had served its purpose. Cervoni's brigade had escaped intact. The trap Beaulieu tried to spring had caught nothing.

That same night, Napoleon was making final arrangements to move three divisions inland. The campaign was about to begin — and the soldiers from Voltri would fight in it.

## The Road to Montenotte {#montenotte}

### The Campaign Ignites

With Beaulieu stuck on the coast near Genoa, Napoleon struck at the exposed center:

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| **11 April** | Argenteau attacks French redoubt at Monte Negino. **Colonel Rampon**: *"Here, we must conquer or die."* Holds through the night. |
| **12 April** | **Battle of Montenotte** — Laharpe attacks frontally, Masséna night-marches to outflank. Argenteau routed. Napoleon's first victory. |
| **13 April** | Battle of Millesimo — Augereau drives back Piedmontese |
| **13-14 April** | Battle of Cosseria — Fierce fight at old castle. Colonel of 18th killed; 26-year-old Suchet takes command. |
| **14-15 April** | Battles of Dego — Masséna takes it; hungry French plunder; Vukassovich retakes at dawn; another day of heavy combat. |
| **21 April** | Battle of Mondovì — Piedmontese routed. **French soldiers finally eat and rest.** |
| **28 April** | Armistice with Piedmont — Napoleon ends the four-year war in less than three weeks. |

### The Soldier's Arc

A soldier from Voltri would have experienced:
1. The humiliation of retreat → then the thrill of being part of Napoleon's counter-stroke
2. Grueling mountain marches in rain and fog
3. A cascade of battles, each bloodier than the last
4. The transformation from a starving garrison soldier into a veteran of a victorious army
5. Finally, descending into the fertile Piedmont plain — where there was bread, wine, and shoes

## Terrain & Geography {#terrain}

### Voltri

A small coastal town **17 km (9 miles) west of Genoa**. In 1796, a separate settlement (now a Genoa suburb). Historically a center of paper production. Stone buildings, narrow streets, a church. The divisional supply train would have been parked here.

### What a Soldier Sees

- **South**: The blue Mediterranean. Fishing boats, possibly distant British sails. April sun on the water.
- **North**: Steep green hillsides rising sharply. Terraced olive groves, Mediterranean scrub (maquis). Mountain passes hidden in folds of terrain. Austrian campfire smoke on the heights.
- **East**: The road to Pegli and Genoa, hugging the coastline.
- **West**: The coastal road to Savona — the lifeline, the escape route. Hills pressing close to the sea.

### Key Terrain Features

- **Turchino Pass** (588m) — Between Masone and Mele. Sebottendorf's approach route. Road winds through narrow valleys.
- **Bocchetta Pass** — 16 km north of Genoa. Pittoni's route, descending from Campomorone.
- **Pegli** — Coastal settlement between Voltri and Genoa. Low hills with defensive positions. Where the 75th fought.
- **Mele** — Inland village on the Turchino road. Had a fortification. Where Lannes and the 51st defended.
- **Colle di Cadibona** (436m) — The lowest pass in the Italian mountain chain. Behind Savona. The strategic hinge Napoleon exploited — not relevant to Voltri directly but critical to what comes next.

### The Coastal Road (Cornice Road)

The retreat route from Voltri to Savona (~40 km). Narrow, winding along the coast. Mountains on one side, sea on the other. The army's lifeline — and its vulnerability. British warships offshore could threaten it.

### Weather

April on the Ligurian coast: typically 11-18°C (52-64°F), ~8 rainy days. But 1796 was colder than usual. The mountains were cold and damp. The coast milder but still uncomfortable for men in rags. Masséna's night march to Montenotte (11-12 April) was through rain and fog.

## Equipment & Weapons {#equipment}

### The Charleville Model 1777 Musket

The standard infantry weapon:
- **Caliber**: .69 (17.5mm), firing undersized .63-.65 ball (easier loading with dirty barrel)
- **Length**: ~60 inches (152 cm) overall, 44.7-inch barrel
- **Weight**: ~10 pounds (4.5 kg)
- **Effective range**: 50-100 yards aimed; volley fire at 100-200 yards (poor accuracy)
- **Rate of fire**: 2-3 rounds per minute (trained soldier)
- **Bayonet**: Triangular socket, ~15 inches, turning musket into 6-foot spear
- **Misfires**: 15-25% failure rate (damp powder, worn flints, fouled touch-holes)

Loading sequence: bite cartridge → prime pan → close frizzen → pour powder → ram ball and wadding → cock → aim → fire.

**Ammunition was chronically short.** Soldiers were supposed to carry 50-60 rounds. Many had far fewer. Replacement flints scarce. Live-fire training essentially impossible.

### Uniforms (or Lack Thereof)

Regulation: blue coat with white facings and red collar, white waistcoat/breeches, black gaiters, bicorne hat, shoes.

Reality in the Army of Italy: coats patched and faded, supplemented with civilian clothing. Trousers whatever could be found. Hats replaced by civilian headgear or captured items. **Shoes were the critical deficiency** — many marched barefoot or with rags on their feet.

> Austrian observers described the French as looking more like bandits than a regular army.

### Other Equipment

- Cartridge box (giberne) on crossbelt
- Bayonet in scabbard on waist belt
- Knapsack (sac) — spare clothing (if any), personal items, rations (if any)
- Water canteen (bidon)
- Haversack for bread/food
- Short sword/briquet — more tool than weapon
- Musket cleaning kit (worm, brush, spare flints, oil)

Many soldiers were missing one or more items. Nothing was standardized in practice.

## The Soldier's Daily Life {#daily-life}

### A Day in Garrison (Late March - Early April 1796)

**Morning**: Réveil at dawn. Roll call — sergent counts heads, notes who deserted in the night, who's sick. Company musters at 40-60% of paper strength. Breakfast: if anything, hard bread and thin soup. Coffee is a luxury. Wine more available than bread on the Ligurian coast.

**Daytime**: Guard duty (long, boring). Foraging parties (buying or requisitioning at bayonet-point from local farmers — olives, chestnuts, figs). Fatigue duties (hauling supplies, repairing fortifications, cutting firewood). Some drill, but morale too low for much formal training.

**The boredom**: Gambling (cards, dice) with worthless stakes. Telling stories, singing revolutionary songs. Complaining about food, officers, the government, the weather. Writing letters if literate (many weren't). Debating the Revolution — many still believed fervently in the Republic. Republican soldiers were notably political.

**Evening**: Campfires — the social center. Veterans of Toulon, Valmy, Jemappes held court. The camaraderie of shared suffering. Soldiers on the coast could see the Mediterranean, some fished to supplement rations. Sleep came early.

### The Ligurian Setting

Dramatically beautiful — steep mountains dropping to the sea, terraced hillsides with olive groves and vineyards, picturesque fishing villages. The irony of starving in paradise would not have been lost on the soldiers.

Genoa's merchant aristocracy was deeply suspicious of these ragged French republicans but couldn't resist their demands. The Genoese tried to maintain neutrality, which satisfied no one. British warships offshore added constant menace.
